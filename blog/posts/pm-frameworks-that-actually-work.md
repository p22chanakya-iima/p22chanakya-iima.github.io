# 3 PM Frameworks That Actually Work (And 3 That Don't)

After years of trying every product management framework in the book, I've become both more opinionated and more pragmatic. Most frameworks are tools — useful in the right context, harmful when applied dogmatically.

Here are the three I keep coming back to, and three I've largely abandoned.

## Frameworks That Work

### 1. Jobs-to-be-Done (JTBD)

**Why it works:** It forces you to think about what the customer is *actually trying to accomplish*, not what they say they want.

The classic example: "People don't want a quarter-inch drill. They want a quarter-inch hole." JTBD takes this further — they probably want to hang a shelf. Maybe they want their living room to feel organized. Understanding the job at each level changes what you build.

**How I use it:** Before writing any PRD, I complete this sentence: *"When [situation], I want to [motivation], so I can [outcome]."* If I can't fill this in clearly, I don't understand the problem yet.

### 2. Opportunity Solution Trees (Teresa Torres)

**Why it works:** It creates a clear visual map from desired outcome to the experiments you're running, with opportunities and solutions as intermediate layers.

Most teams jump from "we need to improve retention" to "let's add push notifications." The OST forces you to map the opportunities in between and evaluate multiple solutions for each.

**How I use it:** I maintain a living OST for my current focus area. It lives in a shared doc, not a fancy tool. The value is in the thinking, not the format.

### 3. The One-Pager PRD

**Why it works:** Constraint breeds clarity. If you can't explain what you're building and why in one page, you don't understand it well enough.

My one-pager format:
- **Problem** (2-3 sentences)
- **Evidence** (data or quotes proving the problem exists)
- **Solution** (what we're building, briefly)
- **Success metrics** (how we'll know it worked)
- **Open questions** (what we still need to figure out)

That's it. No 20-page specs. No feature lists. Just the essence.

## Frameworks That Don't Work (For Me)

### 1. RICE Scoring

**Why it fails:** It gives a false sense of objectivity. You're estimating Reach, Impact, Confidence, and Effort — all subjective numbers. Garbage in, garbage out, but now with a formula that makes everyone feel scientific.

I've seen teams spend hours debating whether something is a "2" or a "3" on Impact. That time is better spent talking to users.

**What I do instead:** Gut-check prioritization with the team, informed by user research. Faster, more honest.

### 2. The Lean Canvas (for mature products)

**Why it fails at scale:** It's brilliant for early-stage startups exploring a new market. But for a PM at an established company working on an existing product, filling out a Business Model Canvas for a notification feature is theater.

**When I still use it:** Only for genuinely new product bets or side projects where the business model is uncertain.

### 3. Strict Agile Ceremonies

**Why it fails:** Not Agile the philosophy — that's great. I mean the rigid ceremony: daily standups at exactly 9:15, sprint planning poker, retrospectives that feel like therapy sessions.

**What I do instead:** Async updates, flexible planning cycles, retros only when something went wrong or surprisingly right. The goal is shipping, not performing process.

## The Meta-Framework

Here's what I've learned: **the best framework is the one your team will actually use consistently.** A imperfect system that everyone follows beats a perfect system that lives in a slide deck.

Pick 2-3 tools. Practice them until they're instinct. Discard the rest.
